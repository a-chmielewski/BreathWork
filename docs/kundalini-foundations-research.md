# Kundalini Foundations research

Research date: 2026-09-07  
Scope: terminology, beginner methods, sequencing, demonstration assets, safety, and optional traditional context for the proposed BreathWork content.

## Research boundaries

This note keeps three kinds of statement separate:

1. **Historical/traditional description** reports what a named text, school, or organization teaches. Terms such as *prana*, *nadi*, *chakra*, and *kundalini* belong here; they are not presented as anatomical structures or medical mechanisms.
2. **Medical evidence** reports findings and limits from clinical, physiological, or public-health sources. It does not validate a whole tradition or every claim made by a tradition source.
3. **Product recommendation** is an editorial decision for BreathWork. Proposed timings are deliberately conservative product choices, not prescriptions found in an ancient text or clinically validated treatment protocols.

The strongest usable sources were official lineage or yoga-organization instructions, a public-domain translation of the *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, US government public-health guidance, and peer-reviewed studies/reviews. Sources were checked for the precise method they used; evidence about one breath pattern is not treated as evidence about another.

## Key planning conclusions

- Name the gentle technique **“Alternate Nostril Breathing (Nadi Shodhana) — no holds.”** The explicit “no holds” qualifier matters because classical and modern sources use the same or overlapping names for versions that include retention.
- Define **Equal Breathing (Sama Vritti)** operationally as a comfortable 1:1 inhale/exhale with smooth transitions and no holds. Do not call it box breathing. Do not make “coherent” a synonym: coherent/resonance breathing is rate-defined, usually near 5.5–6 breaths/minute and sometimes individually assessed, while Sama Vritti is ratio-defined.
- Teach **Dirga/Dirgha (Three-Part Breath)** as one continuous, gentle inhalation noticed sequentially at belly movement, lower ribs, and upper chest, followed by one smooth exhalation. It is not three forceful inhalations and not a physiological sigh.
- A credible **Ujjayi tutorial needs a real human breath-sound demonstration** if the product claims to teach the technique. The sound is part of the method and cannot be conveyed reliably by a phase chime or written metaphor alone. Ujjayi need not be in the first guided sequence.
- A suitable introductory sequence is **Arrive → Dirga → Equal Breathing → no-hold alternate nostril → natural rest**, approximately 9.5–10 minutes. The timings below are editorial starting points and should stay adjustable downward.
- Keep **Breath of Fire out of the introductory sequence**. 3HO, a modern Kundalini Yoga organization in the “Kundalini Yoga as taught by Yogi Bhajan” lineage, explicitly teaches it as foundational, but its method is rapid and coordination-sensitive. It is not interchangeable with classical Bhastrika, and current medical evidence does not establish a safe universal consumer protocol.
- Do not ship a timer-only Breath of Fire exercise. A first release would require an original or properly licensed human demonstration, method review by a qualified teacher familiar with the specifically named lineage, explicit stop/contraindication copy, and a reviewed source-specific work/rest structure. The research does not justify inventing duration, rounds, or progression.
- Offer spiritual/traditional concepts only as optional, attributed context. Do not promise cleansing, chakra activation, nervous-system strengthening, detoxification, trauma treatment, or disease benefit.

## 1. Nadi Shodhana and a no-hold alternate-nostril variation

### Historical/traditional description

The public-domain Pancham Sinh translation of the *Hatha Yoga Pradipika* describes alternating nostrils in chapter 2, verses 7–10, but includes breath confinement/retention and a gradual regimen [S1, S1a]. It also warns that breath should be controlled gradually and that hasty or forceful practice can cause harm. This is evidence that alternate-nostril practice has a historical basis, but **not** that the classical procedure is the no-hold beginner version proposed here.

Contemporary lineage sources use overlapping labels:

- Himalayan Institute calls *nadi shodhana* “channel purification” and explicitly describes a basic form with equal-duration inhale/exhale and “without a pause” [S2, S3].
- Kripalu uses “Nadi Shodhana (Alternate-Nostril Breath)” for the familiar cycle: inhale left, exhale right, inhale right, exhale left, with smooth, soft, comfortable breathing and no stated hold [S4].
- 3HO uses the spelling “Nadi Sodhan Pranayam” and gives the same no-hold cycle in its general pranayam guide [S5].
- *Anuloma Viloma*, *Nadi Shuddhi*, *Nadi Shodhana*, and “alternate nostril breathing” are sometimes used as synonyms, but not consistently. Some sources reserve a name for a particular ratio or retention stage; others do not. The method must therefore be stated, not inferred from the title.

Traditional explanations describe *ida*, *pingala*, and *sushumna* as subtle channels and the practice as “purifying” or balancing their flow [S1, S2, S6]. These are attributed yogic concepts, not established anatomy. Claims that nostril alternation balances brain hemispheres or produces a specific emotional effect should not be presented as medical fact.

### Medical evidence

Small studies have examined alternate-nostril breathing, but methods, populations, durations, and outcomes vary. One trial in healthy participants found no rise in intraocular pressure after alternate-nostril breathing; that narrow finding does not establish broad safety or efficacy for every variation or clinical population [S7]. Older cardiorespiratory studies are small and should not support treatment claims.

The better-supported general safety position is modest: gentle breathing without force or retention is lower intensity than forceful pranayama, but users should still stop if symptoms occur. NCCIH advises beginners to avoid forceful breathing and advises people who are pregnant, older, or have health conditions to discuss individual needs with a health professional and instructor [S8].

### Product recommendation

Use this exact scope label:

> **Alternate Nostril Breathing (Nadi Shodhana) — no holds**

Beginner method:

1. Sit supported, with face, jaw, shoulders, and belly relaxed.
2. Close the right nostril gently; inhale through the left.
3. Switch; exhale through the right.
4. Inhale through the right.
5. Switch; exhale through the left. This is one round.
6. Continue at a comfortable, unforced pace, with no deliberate pause after inhale or exhale.
7. Return both hands to rest and breathe naturally.

Do not prescribe a fixed count on first exposure. If counting is offered, it should adapt to the user’s easy breath rather than lengthening it. Include a hands-free visualization option for congestion, a deviated septum, limited hand mobility, or discomfort, as Kripalu does [S4]. Never tell a user to force air through a blocked nostril.

Copy should say “stop and return to natural breathing if you feel dizzy, breathless, panicky, numb/tingly, or uncomfortable.” Persistent or first-time unexplained rapid breathing, chest pain, fainting, or worsening breathing needs medical attention rather than continued breath coaching [S9].

## 2. Sama Vritti / Equal Breathing

### Traditional/modern yoga description

Modern yoga organizations do not use *Sama Vritti* uniformly. Kripalu’s “Samavritti (Balancing Breath or Counting Breath)” uses a one-to-one ratio—for example, smooth inhale for four and smooth exhale for four—for two to five minutes [S4]. A Yoga International article gives a particularly clear no-hold form: inhale for three, four, or five counts, exhale for the same count, keep transitions smooth, and adapt to breath capacity [S10].

Other sources call a four-part inhale–hold–exhale–hold pattern “Sama Vritti,” “square breath,” or “box breath” [S11]. Because of this conflict, the Sanskrit name alone does not define whether holds are present.

### Medical evidence and distinction from Coherent Breathing

For BreathWork, distinguish patterns by their actual timing:

- **Equal Breathing / Sama Vritti:** equal inhale and exhale durations. In this scope it has **no holds**. It does not inherently specify breaths per minute.
- **Box Breathing:** inhale, hold, exhale, hold, with equal or otherwise prescribed phase durations. The 2023 randomized trial by Balban et al. defined its box-breathing arm this way [S12].
- **Coherent/resonance-frequency breathing:** slow paced breathing near cardiovascular resonance. Research commonly places this near 6 breaths/minute; individual resonance frequencies in one study typically ranged 4.5–7 breaths/minute, with 5.5 most common [S13, S14].

Equal duration does not automatically mean coherent rate. A 4-second inhale plus 4-second exhale is 7.5 breaths/minute. A 5.5-second inhale plus 5.5-second exhale is about 5.45 breaths/minute and happens to fall near commonly studied resonance rates, but the concepts remain different: one specifies **ratio**, the other **overall rate/physiological target**. Individual resonance-frequency assessment also uses physiological measurement; a generic app pacer should not imply it has found a user’s personal resonance.

Slow-breathing evidence is heterogeneous and generally concerns rates around 4–10 breaths/minute, not the Sanskrit label itself [S14]. It supports cautious language such as “paced slow breathing has measurable cardiorespiratory effects,” not promises to treat anxiety, blood pressure, or autonomic dysfunction.

### Product recommendation

Use:

> **Equal Breathing (Sama Vritti): inhale and exhale for the same comfortable length, with no holds.**

Recommended first-use cue:

> Let the inhale and exhale become equal and easy. Try 3 in / 3 out or 4 in / 4 out only if comfortable. Shorten the count or return to natural breathing at any time.

Avoid:

- “square,” “box,” or a four-sided visual, because these imply retention;
- “Coherent Breathing” as an alternate title;
- claims that 4/4 is a medically optimal rate;
- “deepest possible” inhalation. Comfortable, smooth breathing matters more than volume.

## 3. Dirga/Dirgha / Three-Part Breath

### Traditional/modern yoga description

Kripalu describes Three-Part Breath (*Dirgha pranayama*) as a gentle wave: awareness progresses from soft belly expansion to rib-cage expansion and then the upper chest/clavicle area [S15]. Its home-practice guidance says to fill “belly, rib cage, and collarbones with a smooth, wave-like breath” and avoid strain or force [S16].

3HO’s “Long Deep Breathing” teaching uses the same basic sequencing: belly expands, then ribs, then upper chest; exhale reverses upper, middle, lower; after learning the regions separately, blend them into one seamless cycle [S5]. That makes it relevant to a Kundalini Foundations collection, though “Long Deep Breathing” is the organization’s preferred name.

An anatomical wording correction is important: air does not enter the belly. The lungs remain in the thorax. Belly movement is an external sign of diaphragmatic descent and abdominal displacement during inhalation [S14]. Product copy should cue **movement or awareness** in the belly, not claim that the belly fills with air.

### Distinction from three forceful inhalations and physiological sigh

The complete practice is one continuous inhalation with sequential awareness, not three separately restarted inhalations. The staged “part one / part two / part three” teaching in some tutorials is a learning progression before combining the regions, not an instruction to gulp three times.

It is also not cyclic sighing or a “physiological sigh.” In Balban et al., cyclic sighing used one slow inhale followed by a second inhale to fill further, then a slow full exhale [S12]. That double-inhale mechanics and exhale emphasis are materially different from Dirga’s one wave-like inhale. Evidence from the cyclic-sighing trial must not be used as evidence for Dirga.

### Product recommendation

Use a non-maximal cue:

> Inhale gently as you notice the belly soften outward, the lower ribs widen, and the upper chest receive the end of the same smooth breath. Exhale in one easy wave. Keep the throat, jaw, and shoulders relaxed.

The UI should animate one inhale arc with three subtle awareness markers, not three inhale pulses. Avoid “fill completely,” “pack in more air,” forceful abdominal squeezing, or a second top-up inhale. Let users omit the upper-chest emphasis if it creates strain.

## 4. Ujjayi method and demonstration asset

### Historical/traditional description

The *Hatha Yoga Pradipika* describes Ujjayi as narrowing/closing the laryngeal passage so inhalation makes a sound, followed by retention and exhalation through the left nostril [S1]. This historical description differs from the common modern, no-hold, two-nostril tutorial and should not be silently presented as the same complete protocol.

Modern Himalayan Institute guidance describes slow, deep inhalation and exhalation with partial glottic closure and an even, continuous sound [S3]. Yoga International gives a practical beginner version: sit neutrally, gently narrow the throat as if saying “aah” without vocalizing and then close the mouth, breathe diaphragmatically through the nose, maintain the light narrowing on inhale and exhale, and keep both phases smooth with no pause [S17]. Kripalu similarly describes a steady nasal breath with mild throat constriction and an “ocean” sound, while warning against strain or force [S16, S18].

The modern sound is variously described as oceanic, hissing, whisper-like, or sobbing. These metaphors can produce very different intensity. The safest product target is **soft and private-audible**, not theatrical “Darth Vader” volume.

### Medical evidence

These sources establish how their traditions teach Ujjayi; they do not establish broad medical efficacy. Claims about “stimulating the vagus,” heating the body, detoxifying, or treating disease should not be used without technique-specific clinical support. Throat pain, air hunger, dizziness, or strain are reasons to release the constriction and breathe naturally.

### Product recommendation and asset decision

A real demonstration is **required for a tutorial that claims to teach Ujjayi**, for four reasons:

1. Sound quality is a defining feedback signal.
2. Text metaphors do not communicate loudness, smoothness, or the difference between gentle glottic narrowing and harsh throat tension.
3. A synthetic whoosh or phase cue demonstrates timing, not human airway technique.
4. A real clip can demonstrate both nasal inhale and nasal exhale at an appropriately quiet level.

Preferred asset:

- a short original recording by a qualified instructor;
- clear consent/model release and documented recording ownership;
- a close, dry recording without music or heavy processing;
- one slow open-mouth “aah” rehearsal, then mouth-closed nasal Ujjayi;
- front/side video or simple animation showing relaxed jaw, neck, shoulders, and belly;
- captions/transcript and a visual fallback;
- normalized playback that does not make the breath seem louder than intended.

Do not copy audio from an online tutorial without an explicit compatible license. If no real demonstration can be sourced, keep Ujjayi as attributed reading/context and do not claim the app teaches correct execution.

Ujjayi should be a standalone optional tutorial, not another required technique in the first multi-stage session.

## 5. Introductory guided sequence

### Proposed sequence

This is an editorial composition informed by the lower ends of modern organization guidance, not a quoted traditional kriya or medical protocol:

1. **Arrive — 1:30**
   - Supported seat or chair; eyes open, lowered, or closed by choice.
   - Notice contact points and natural breath. Relax jaw, shoulders, and belly.
2. **Dirga / Three-Part Breath — 2:00**
   - First 30–45 seconds: notice belly and lower-rib movement.
   - Then one smooth belly → ribs → upper-chest awareness wave.
   - No holds and no maximal inhalation.
3. **Equal Breathing — 2:00**
   - Offer 3/3 or 4/4 only as optional comfortable counts.
   - No holds. “Shorter or natural” remains visible.
4. **Alternate Nostril Breathing (Nadi Shodhana), no holds — 2:00**
   - Approximately 4–6 unhurried rounds, but end by comfort/time rather than forcing a round count.
   - Complete the current exhale before releasing the hand.
5. **Natural rest — 2:00**
   - No pacing target. Let breathing return to itself.
   - Notice effects without telling the user what they should feel.

Nominal total: **9:30**. Allow transition copy to bring the experience to about **10:00** without extending controlled breathing.

### Why this order

- Arrival establishes choice, posture, and a baseline before control.
- Dirga introduces movement awareness before numerical pacing.
- Equal Breathing simplifies attention to one ratio without adding nostril coordination.
- Alternate-nostril breathing comes after the user has found a smooth rhythm.
- Natural rest prevents an abrupt finish and avoids implying that continuous control is the goal.

Kripalu’s examples use two to five minutes for Samavritti and three to five minutes for Dirga and Nadi Shodhana [S4, S16]. The shorter two-minute product stages are conservative because this sequence combines several unfamiliar techniques in one session. They are not claims of an optimal dose.

### Session-wide safety and accessibility

- Always provide **Pause**, **Rest now**, and immediate **Stop**.
- “Rest now” should enter unpaced natural breathing, not another exercise.
- Do not use retention, forceful breathing, or Breath of Fire in this sequence.
- Keep posture choice-based; do not require lotus pose or a perfectly straight spine.
- Let users keep eyes open and skip interoceptive/body-focused prompts.
- Avoid treatment language. The session is educational/wellness content, not a substitute for medical or mental-health care.
- If a user becomes dizzy, faint, panicky, numb/tingly, unusually breathless, or uncomfortable, instruct them to stop deliberate pacing and breathe naturally. First-time or persistent rapid breathing, chest pain, fainting, or worsening symptoms should be medically assessed [S9].

## 6. Breath of Fire, Bhastrika, demonstration, pacing, and safety

### Source tradition and method

3HO International describes Breath of Fire (*agni praan/pranayam*) as a foundational/signature practice in **“Kundalini Yoga as taught by Yogi Bhajan”** [S19]. 3HO is therefore direct evidence for how that specific modern organization teaches the method. It is not evidence that every historical or contemporary Kundalini tradition uses the term or method identically.

3HO’s current instruction specifies:

- rapid, rhythmic, continuous nasal breathing with mouth closed;
- equal inhale and exhale, with no pause;
- approximately 2–3 cycles per second;
- active exhalation from inward/upward navel movement;
- inhalation arising from abdominal release rather than a second forceful effort;
- chest, face, neck, and shoulders kept relaxed [S19, S5].

3HO explicitly says its Breath of Fire is not Bellows Breath [S19]. A Kripalu article describing Kundalini Yoga likewise presents Breath of Fire as a light, even, quick nasal breath with equal inhale/exhale and natural belly movement, showing that even modern teaching language varies in how strongly it cues abdominal force [S20].

### Distinction from Bhastrika and terminology conflict

In the *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, Bhastrika is explicitly compared with a blacksmith’s bellows: air is forcefully drawn in and expelled repeatedly, followed by a right-nostril inhale, retention, and left-nostril exhale in that translation [S1]. This is not the same mechanics as 3HO’s active-exhale/release-inhale Breath of Fire.

Terminology remains conflicted:

- 3HO: Breath of Fire is **not** Bellows Breath/Bhastrika [S19].
- Art of Living calls Bhastrika the “yogic breath of fire” [S21].
- Some modern classes use “Breath of Fire” for Kapalabhati or group several rapid breaths together.

Therefore every Breath of Fire screen, source note, and asset should name the lineage and spell out mechanics. Do not use “Breath of Fire/Bhastrika” as a combined title.

### Medical evidence and uncertainty

There is no strong evidence in the reviewed sources establishing an optimal or universally safe 3HO Breath of Fire dose for an unsupervised consumer app.

Relevant evidence is indirect:

- In a small study of 20 healthy participants, Bhastrika and Kapalabhati caused acute increases in several cardiovascular measures during practice [S22]. Because those methods are not identical to 3HO Breath of Fire, this supports caution around rapid/forceful breathing but cannot be transferred as a quantified Breath of Fire effect.
- A 2025 systematic review of pranayama for mental disorders found overall high risk of bias, insufficient safety reporting, and more adverse events with fast than slow techniques. Reported minor events included anxiety, breathlessness, cough, dizziness, constriction, flashback, headache, and neck pain; much of the included intervention evidence concerned multi-part protocols, not isolated 3HO Breath of Fire [S23].
- NCCIH advises beginners to avoid forceful breathing and recommends qualified instruction and individualized modification for pregnancy, older adults, and health conditions [S8].
- MedlinePlus explains that rapid deep overbreathing can lower carbon dioxide and produce lightheadedness, breathlessness, chest symptoms, tingling, or spasms [S9].

3HO says Breath of Fire is “not hyperventilation,” yet the same page acknowledges tingling and lightheadedness and attributes some sensations to “toxins” [S19]. **Do not repeat the toxin explanation.** From a medical standpoint, whether rapid breathing produces hypocapnia depends on ventilation, not the practice’s traditional name. Symptoms should be treated as stop signals, not normalization or evidence that the technique is working.

### Method-specific safety attribution

3HO’s own contraindication list says to refrain:

- during pregnancy;
- during the first or second days of menstruation depending on flow;
- with dizziness, panic, or uncontrolled high blood pressure;
- while recovering from surgery or serious illness [S19].

These are **3HO’s lineage-specific instructions**, not a complete medical contraindication list. NCCIH separately flags severe high blood pressure, glaucoma, pregnancy, older age, and other health conditions as reasons to seek individual modification for yoga practice generally [S8]. A consumer product should not turn either list into a claim that everyone outside it is safe.

Additional conservative product exclusions/referrals should be reviewed by a clinician and qualified lineage teacher before release, especially for cardiovascular or pulmonary disease, seizure disorders, glaucoma/retinal conditions, recent abdominal or thoracic surgery, panic/PTSD susceptibility, and medications affecting heart rate or blood pressure. This research identifies these as review questions, not a finalized protocol.

### Demonstration requirement

Breath of Fire needs more than an animation or timer. A release-quality tutorial should include:

- a real instructor demonstrating abdomen, relaxed upper chest, face, neck, and shoulders;
- audible rhythm at normal playback speed;
- a slow-motion teaching breakdown that is clearly labeled **demonstration only**, not the final prescribed pace;
- side-by-side correction for paradoxical/reverse breathing;
- explicit distinction from forceful inhalation/Bhastrika and from large-volume hyperventilation;
- always-visible stop signals and natural-breath recovery;
- captions, transcript, and non-audio cues;
- documented instructor qualification, lineage, consent, and asset rights.

### Pacing, duration, and rest recommendation

Do **not** derive a beginner dose from 3HO’s broad “1 to 31 minutes” range or automatically set a pacer to 2–3 cycles/second [S19]. Those statements describe the organization’s practice, not a medically established onboarding protocol.

The reviewed sources do not provide a sufficiently supported universal answer for:

- beginner cycle rate;
- first-session duration;
- rounds;
- rest interval;
- progression criteria;
- safe resumption after symptoms.

Accordingly:

1. Exclude Breath of Fire from the introductory guided sequence.
2. Do not invent a rounds-and-rest protocol.
3. Before implementation, obtain a source-authored, lineage-specific beginner progression from a qualified instructor and have its safety copy reviewed clinically.
4. End any reviewed round with unpaced natural breathing and do not resume while symptoms remain. If symptoms occur, the session should stop rather than coach the user through a performance target.
5. If those dependencies cannot be met, keep Breath of Fire as attributed educational context only.

## 7. Optional spiritual and traditional context

These concepts can be offered in a collapsed, opt-in “Traditional context” section:

- **Prana:** described in yoga traditions as vital energy or life force; pranayama is framed as working with it [S5, S6]. Attribute this to the tradition and do not equate prana with oxygen, carbon dioxide, nerves, electricity, or a measurable medical substance.
- **Nadis:** subtle channels in yogic models. *Nadi shodhana* is commonly glossed as channel purification/cleansing [S2, S6]. Do not depict nadis as blood vessels or literal nerves.
- **Ida, pingala, sushumna:** named subtle channels associated in yoga sources with left/right/central flow [S1, S2, S6]. Avoid claiming verified hemisphere control.
- **Kundalini and chakras/navel point:** 3HO and the *Hatha Yoga Pradipika* use these concepts to explain practice aims [S1, S19]. Present them as lineage teachings, not mechanisms of disease treatment.
- **Mantra and class frame:** 3HO asks practitioners to “tune in” with *Ong Namo Guru Dev Namo* and closes practice with the “Long Time Sun” song [S19]. If included, identify this specifically as 3HO/Yogi Bhajan-lineage framing, make it optional, and clear music/recording rights independently.
- **Witnessing/rest:** noticing sensations without requiring a predicted outcome is compatible with modern teaching sources and avoids turning traditional meaning into an efficacy promise [S16].

Recommended attribution pattern:

> In [named source/tradition], this practice is understood as …  
> This is a traditional framework, not a medical claim.

Avoid:

- “scientifically proven ancient technology”;
- detoxification or “cleansing the blood”;
- awakening kundalini as a promised product outcome;
- guaranteed chakra, endocrine, immune, trauma, anxiety, or blood-pressure effects;
- presenting one modern organization as the single historical Kundalini tradition.

## 8. Uncertainty and terminology log

- **Nadi Shodhana / Nadi Shuddhi / Nadi Sodhan / Anuloma Viloma:** overlapping modern usage; some versions include retention or ratios and others do not. Always append the actual phase pattern.
- **Sama Vritti / Samavritti:** often means equal inhale/exhale, but some sources use it for four-part square/box breathing. BreathWork should define its no-hold version every time.
- **Coherent Breathing:** not a traditional synonym for Sama Vritti. Research definitions emphasize overall rate near cardiovascular resonance; commercial programs may use the label more loosely.
- **Dirga / Dirgha:** both transliterations appear. “Three-Part Breath” may describe either progressive teaching of three regions or the integrated wave. BreathWork should show one integrated inhale after the learning cues.
- **Ujjayi:** the classical translated text includes retention and one-nostril exhalation; common modern instruction uses continuous two-nostril breathing without holds. Label the app’s version “modern introductory, no holds.”
- **Breath of Fire:** 3HO’s method is lineage-specific and explicitly distinguished from Bellows Breath, while other organizations use “breath of fire” for Bhastrika or Kapalabhati. Never merge the names.
- **Safety evidence:** adverse-event reporting in pranayama studies is weak. Absence of reported harm is not proof of safety. Fast-breathing evidence is particularly difficult to generalize because protocols are often combined and terminology is inconsistent.
- **Efficacy evidence:** slow-breathing studies support measurable physiological effects under defined conditions, but do not validate spiritual mechanisms or justify disease-treatment claims for these product lessons.

**[S20]** Kripalu, “3 Revitalizing Kundalini Yoga Techniques.” Contemporary description of a light, even Breath of Fire variation; useful for showing variation in teaching cues.

### Medical, physiological, and public-health sources

**[S7]** Venkatesh et al., “The Effect of Specific Techniques of Nasal Breathing [Pranayama] on Intra-Ocular Pressure in Normal Individuals, a Randomized Trial,” PubMed record. Narrow, short-term study in participants without glaucoma; not broad efficacy evidence.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36532821/

**[S8]** US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, “Yoga: Effectiveness and Safety.” Federal public-health guidance; qualified instruction, beginners avoiding forceful breathing, and individualized modification.  
https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/yoga-effectiveness-and-safety

**[S9]** MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia, “Hyperventilation.” US National Library of Medicine patient guidance on rapid/deep overbreathing, symptoms, and when to seek care.  
https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003071.htm

**[S12]** Balban et al., “Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal,” *Cell Reports Medicine* 4 (2023), article 100895. Original randomized remote trial; used to define cyclic sighing and box breathing and to distinguish them from Dirga/Equal Breathing.  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895

Open full text:  
https://www.cell.com/cell-reports-medicine/fulltext/S2666-3791(22)00474-8

**[S13]** Steffen et al., “The Impact of Resonance Frequency Breathing on Measures of Heart Rate Variability, Blood Pressure, and Mood,” *Frontiers in Public Health* 5 (2017). Original randomized study; defines individually assessed resonance frequency and reports the typical range.  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5575449/

**[S14]** Russo, Santarelli, and O’Rourke, “The physiological effects of slow breathing in the healthy human,” *Breathe* 13 (2017). Clinical physiology review; defines slow breathing as 4–10 breaths/minute and reviews resonance near 6 breaths/minute and respiratory biomechanics.  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5709795/

Publisher version:  
https://breathe.ersjournals.com/content/13/4/298

**[S22]** Kuppusamy et al., “Evaluation of Cardiovascular Functions during the Practice of Different Types of Yogic Breathing Techniques,” PubMed record. Small study in 20 healthy participants; relevant only as indirect evidence that rapid Bhastrika/Kapalabhati have acute cardiovascular effects.

**[S24]** Cramer et al., “Adverse Events Associated with Yoga: A Systematic Review of Published Case Reports and Case Series,” *PLOS ONE* 8 (2013). Low-level harms evidence based on case reports/series; supports caution and qualified instruction but cannot estimate incidence.
